I was talking with someone recently about what the internet looked like when it first appeared.

No mobile payments, no food delivery, no e-commerce, no short video. People looking at the internet then probably could not explain how it would change daily life. Today, a life without those things is impossible to imagine.

I keep feeling that AI is standing in the same place right now: the models exist, the frameworks exist, but nobody can really see what "e-commerce on top of AI" or "food delivery on top of AI" will be.

## A history of outsourcing

If I had to compress economic history into one word, I would choose outsourcing.

Before the industrial revolution, almost all value came from muscle. Then humans outsourced muscle to machines, stepped behind them, and used their brains to design and maintain the machines. Productivity grew exponentially for the first time.

The information revolution outsourced the next layer: humans wrote business processes and organisational management into software, let code execute the processes, and let the processes direct the machines. We stopped moving information ourselves and started writing rules for systems to move it.

Along this line, AI is just another layer of outsourcing — but it covers the last piece, thinking itself. We used to outsource hands and processes; now we are starting to outsource judgement, writing and analysis, the parts that used to require a person.

That is why I think "will AI replace people" is a badly posed question. Every previous round of outsourcing replaced one layer of labour and pushed people up a layer. The real uncertainty is not whether you will be replaced, but what the new layer requires and who gets there first.

## What year of the internet is AI in?

Nobody can answer that precisely, but there is a useful test.

The people who judged internet directions back then used a similar idea: find a direction such that, in the future, not having it would be as unimaginable as not having e-commerce today. Judging AI today works the same way — do not ask "which industry will AI change", ask "what will become infrastructure that most people have not noticed yet".

That test has a precondition: you have to go deep enough in a vertical to see what is missing. From outside you see excitement; from inside you see exactly where the problem gets stuck.

A few likely directions are already happening, they just have not grown into infrastructure yet: assistance across the whole sales follow-up process, screening and communication inside hiring, turning unstructured documents into an answerable knowledge base. Today each of them is a scattered tool. It is not hard to imagine that in five years a company without them will compete the way a retailer without e-commerce competes today.

## What fits AI

My judgement is fairly conservative. The scenarios that fit AI usually share a few traits.

- The work is repetitive and has a clear feedback signal, so a new version of the model can be judged as better or worse.
- The data can be structured and retrieved, rather than scattered across chat logs and screenshots.
- Mistakes can be verified and rolled back, so one bad output does not cause irreversible damage.
- People still make the judgement at key points; the model prepares material and candidates.

Conversely, scenarios with slow feedback, dirty data, very expensive errors, or responsibility that cannot be transferred will adopt AI much more slowly — and are more likely to become demos than production systems.

> The model sets the ceiling; implementation sets the floor. Most projects fail not because the model is too weak, but because the process was never changed.

## Why implementation beats models

In my last internship I worked on AI implementation, and the biggest takeaway was this: the model is the most standardised part of the whole system.

The real difficulty is elsewhere. Which system does the data come from, how are permissions controlled, who reviews the output, what happens to colleagues who are used to the old process, how do you roll back when it fails. None of it sounds exciting, but it decides whether AI moves from a demo to something used every day.

A concrete example. A sales team wants AI to help write follow-up emails, and the model can already do that. But to actually use it, someone has to answer: where does customer information come from, how do we show the model past emails, who reviews the generated mail, how do we measure whether it worked. The answers to those questions decide whether the AI gets opened once a week or used twenty times a day.

A ten percent better model might improve the final result only slightly. Sorting out the process, connecting the right data and changing people's habits might double the effect. That is why I think the scarce people in the next few years are not the ones who can tune models, but the ones who can connect AI to real work.

## In the end

Humans have always been outsourcing labour, and AI is a larger round in that long process. Its impact will leave the tech bubble and land on every concrete job.

For an individual, rather than being anxious about replacement, it is better to answer two questions early: in my field, which work can be outsourced to AI; and after that outsourcing, what capabilities does the remaining part require?

The answer is not necessarily comforting, but at least it is concrete. The people who benefit most from a wave of outsourcing are rarely the ones who fight it; they are the ones who learn to stand on the side that is doing the outsourcing.
