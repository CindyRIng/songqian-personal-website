This morning I made 42 calls. Nine connected, two added me on WeChat, one agreed to a visit.

In the afternoon, tidying the list, I did the arithmetic. Since starting at Fumamx I have made roughly 2,700 calls and closed three deals. On commission, that is about one yuan per call.

I did not like saying that number at first. Now I find it interesting.

## One yuan a call

I am at my desk at 8:30. The first thing is the call list: company name, contact, phone number, industry — mostly pool customers from the system.

About one in five calls connects. Of those, most say "not needed", "send me some material", or "we already have a system". Two or three WeChat adds a day is a decent day.

Connecting is not the same as useful. Some people pick up, say "busy" after two sentences when they mean "I do not want to talk". Some talk for five minutes and leave nothing behind. I probably get five or six genuinely useful conversations a day; the rest is process.

At the start I cared about closing. Every call was supposed to end in a sale — features, case studies, discounts. It went badly. People hung up after two sentences, and I was exhausted.

Then the colleague who was training me said one thing: a phone is a sieve, not a hammer. Your job is not to persuade. It is to find the people worth visiting.

Now every call does four things: confirm they export; get a sense of the team and products; judge whether there is real pain; book a visit or add WeChat.

Closing happens later. Closing on the phone is mostly fantasy.

## Scripts belong to situations

At the beginning I memorised a standard script: opening, product pitch, objection handling. I knew it well.

It did not survive contact. Every owner is different — some do hardware, some textiles, some only domestic trade and are thinking about exporting. One script gets you through maybe three sentences.

Now I open with three things: do you export; what we do; let me add your WeChat and send some material. The rest is conversation.

Experienced people will find this obvious. It took me until around call one thousand to actually believe it.

## What I look for in a customer

Now I check a few things: do they really export; can this person make the decision; how big is the team, how wide the product line; is there obvious pain right now; do they have a basic idea of what a CRM does; are they planning to adopt something soon.

All six, good customer. Three or four, worth following. None, just add WeChat and move on.

Written down, it looks like a template. In practice the judgement happens in one or two minutes on feel, accurate or not. I still misjudge a fair number. Some customers I was sure about went quiet after two follow-ups.

The reverse happens too: some I thought were average turned out to convert. So now: drop the obvious no's, keep everything else, and do not conclude too early.

## Where the lists come from

Mostly the public pool in the system plus a few open business directories. I screen by industry first and leave the too-mixed ones aside. In Shijiazhuang the exporters are mostly hardware, textiles, chemicals, with some machinery parts.

Before dialling I glance at the company name and scope and form a rough guess. It takes seconds and saves a lot of pointless talking.

## Beyond the phone

The phone is the opening. After a visit is booked, the real work starts: watch their process, ask what they use now, find out who actually uses it, see how data goes in and out.

Last month I started going to the Canton Fair with the team. The customers there are more mixed — the Middle East, South America, Southeast Asia. Many already have systems; the systems just do not talk to each other. That need does not surface on a call. You have to sit down.

## Notes beat scripts

After a call I now write three things: what they said, what they care about, what happens next.

Writing it down showed me something: on the second call, many customers ask the same question as the first. If you do not write it down, you ask again, and they get annoyed.

I wrote a small tool that files call records by customer so I can find them a month later. It is ugly, but it beats memory.

## What I am still bad at

Rejection makes you blunt. At first, every hang-up needed a moment. Now I hang up and dial the next. Whether that is progress or numbness, I cannot say.

Pricing is where I go soft. When a customer asks about cost, I over-talk, and regret it on the way back.

Call notes often wait until the evening, by which point the details are gone. I try to write two lines immediately now; when it gets busy, I still miss them.

There is also rhythm. Mornings are good. After three or four in the afternoon, the quality drops and my voice goes flat. I have not found a fix.

Lately I have been reviewing the deals that did not close. Three went well and chose someone else. I went through the process: one I quoted too late, one I was talking to the wrong level, one I still do not understand. Nobody teaches you this; you look at them one by one.

Tomorrow I dial again. There are still a hundred and some numbers left on the list.
