One winter in my second year, the tennis court was empty except for me. The wind kept pushing my serves sideways. I practised until my fingers went numb. On the walk back to the dorm I thought: maybe I should just quit the club. I was not getting anywhere.

I did not quit. The reason is boring: I had paid the club fee and bought the racket.

Looking back, the things that actually changed me over those four years were not the decisive moments. They were afternoons like that one — show up, play for two hours, see no progress, come back the next day.

## Four years do not pass at a constant speed

In first year I thought there was plenty of time. Few classes, little homework, and the biggest decision of the day was what to eat.

Second year, things started to split. Some people prepared for competitions, some built projects, some began studying for graduate school. I kept going to the court. At the time it felt like wasting time — other people were accumulating things, and I was picking up balls.

By third and fourth year the gap became concrete. Not because anyone was smarter, but because some people had something to show and some did not. I had very little: a club, a few hundred hours of practice, and a graduation project about a ball-collecting robot.

Those few things got me my first job. Not because they were impressive, but because I could explain exactly where they came from.

## The year I was club president

In third year I became club president. Sounds good. In reality nobody else wanted the job.

That year was a pile of small tasks: writing the club charter, reworking the training plan, recruiting, arguing with the court manager, arriving early to hold the courts. The most absurd moment: I sent twenty messages in the group chat to gather people for a match. Six showed up.

I learned more that year than in the previous two combined. How to organise a group, how to deal with the university administration, how to push something forward when nobody else is helping. None of that is on a syllabus.

## Tennis is the most honest compounding

I almost quit many times. Second year I wanted to quit. Third year, busy with the club, I wanted to quit. Fourth year, while job hunting, I wanted to quit.

I stayed every time, for an unromantic reason: I had nothing else to do on Wednesday afternoons.

Some changes were real. In first year I could not return a ball; eight of ten serves went into the net. Now I can stand on a court for an afternoon, hit with freshmen, and roughly read where the opponent's ball is going.

None of this comes with a certificate. None of it goes on a résumé. But the body remembers. It knows how long you have practised. A match knows whether you have been lazy.

It works the same for habits. The fixed Wednesday session later became a fixed weekly writing session and a fixed weekly note-organising session. I have never been good at sticking with things. I learned it from tennis.

> I do not really believe in "find your passion, then work hard." It is more like: do one thing seriously first, and the love grows out of doing it.

## Slow things

For six months my serve did not change. Every week I practised, every week it looked the same. My coach said: forget whether it goes in, just get the toss stable.

I did that for half a year. One afternoon, the serves started landing. Nothing was learned that day. The previous six months had simply reached a threshold.

A lot of things work like that: writing, building tools, learning maths. The early part is long enough to make you doubt yourself. But looking back, those repetitions that seemed useless were all adding something.

Slow feedback is the scarcest thing in university. Courses have exams and homework has grades, but the abilities that actually matter — communication, judgement, patience, physical condition — give you nothing back for a long time. You have to believe it is worth doing on your own.

The graduation project was the same. For two months the robot either hit walls or missed balls; it picked up ten and dropped eight. Change the frame, tune the parameters, swap the motor, again and again. A week before the defence, the success rate reached seventy percent. No single change was decisive. Dozens of small changes were.

## Three things I got right

If I had to summarise, it would be three things.

- I found something I could do every week for four years without getting bored.
- I turned it into something with a concrete result: a club, a match, a graduation project.
- I did not run away when it was slow.

That sounds like a motivational speech. Behind each one is a mess of details: the club almost failed to recruit anyone, I lost a match 0–6, and I was debugging the robot at three in the morning.

## Last

On graduation day I sat at the court until late. I do not know if I will still play every week.

But I know those afternoons were not wasted. Not because they turned into something impressive, but because they showed me that a person can get a little better through one very ordinary thing.

I calculated once: about four hundred sessions over four years, two hours each. That sounds like a lot. Per week it is twice. The hard part was never those two hours. It was the ten minutes before leaving the dorm.

The summer after first year I stayed at school and practised for two weeks. For fourteen days I did one thing. It was the biggest improvement of my four years, and the first time I thought I could keep doing this.

The word "compounding" sounds grand. For me it was just showing up on Wednesday afternoons for four years.
