On one of my last evenings before graduation I went to the tennis court, the way I usually did. It was quiet. The lights came on one by one, the sound of a racket hitting a ball was the same sound it had always been. I stood behind the baseline and realised this might be one of the last times I hit here as a student.

Four years earlier I could barely hold the racket. Back then four years looked long — long enough to spend the first year playing around and think about the future later. Looking back, the four years did not pass evenly at all.

## Four years do not pass evenly

In first year everything felt like "there is plenty of time." Clubs, courses, friends, the future — everything looked possible, and the easiest thing in the world was to believe that I still had lots of time.

The split starts in second year. On the surface everyone was still taking the same classes and joining the same activities, but some people were quietly building a rhythm while others were waiting for the day their condition would improve. You could not see the difference then. Three or four years later it looks like a watershed.

By third and fourth year, what decides your state is no longer what you have thought about but what you have actually done. How you spend a day, where your weeks go, whether you have kept at one thing for a long time — all of it slowly gets written into your body, your abilities and your options.

So the things that stayed with me are not a few moments of excitement, or some big idea I suddenly understood one day. They are ordinary practices, ordinary evenings, repetitions that felt useless at the time.

> It is never too late to work hard, but compounding is better started as early as possible.

## What compounding actually compounds

When people hear "compounding" they usually think of money. In four years I felt three kinds of it.

- Skills compound. Practise a skill for a year and it is still a hobby; practise it seriously for four years and it becomes part of your temperament, your confidence and your choices. Tennis, English, programming, speaking — the gap is not opened in a day; it widens through long repetition.
- The body compounds. The difference between people who keep moving and people who do not becomes obvious after four years. Your body is not an accessory, it is infrastructure: sleep, stamina, emotional stability and the ability to follow through tend to be connected.
- Habits and character compound. Focus compounds, self-discipline compounds, emotional stability compounds, finishing what you start compounds. None of it gets applause at first, but time amplifies it.

So compounding is not about luck. It is about what you leave behind, day after day.

## The hardest part is not the effort — it is not seeing results

Almost everyone goes through three stages: not being able to do it, repeating, and breaking through.

The first stage is not being able to do it. Your movements are stiff, you cannot return the ball, and you suspect you are just not built for this.
The second stage is repetition. Every day you practise roughly the same thing and progress feels like walking in place. It is the most boring stage and the easiest one to quit.
The third stage is the breakthrough. One day you notice the ball is a little steadier and your body seems to know how to cooperate.

Most people fall in the second stage. Not because they lack talent, but because they leave before the results are visible. It took me a while to accept that often you are not failing to improve — you are simply in the stretch before improvement becomes visible.

## Love grows out of doing

I do not really believe in "find your passion first, then work hard."

In first year I played tennis because I wanted an excuse to get out of the dorm. It was not love at the time; it was even a little boring. I only started to like it after many repetitions: a day when training suddenly clicked, a day when I won a long rally in a match, a day when I realised I could already teach someone else.

Love is more like a result than a precondition. Do one thing seriously first; love grows in the doing.

## Keep one small thing for a long time

If someone asked me what was most worth doing in university, my answer is not a certificate or a project. It is: find one concrete small thing and keep doing it for long enough.

Tennis was that thing for me. It is concrete, so it is honest. Your body knows whether you have practised. A match knows whether you have patience. And you know best whether you can face losing.

Eventually I wrote down a few plain rules for myself, and I still use them.

Show up on time, which matters more than one great session. Change one variable at a time; do not try to fix everything in a day. Accept slow, accept repetition, accept that your beginning will not look good. Allow bad stretches, but do not quit easily.

These rules are not only about tennis. They are the most concrete version of what long-term thinking means to me: not burning every day, not being excited every day, but finding a rhythm you can actually keep.

## In the end

Graduation is not the end. The court will not empty out and the club will not stop. Someone new will always come, and someone will carry the love forward.

I only hope that when I look back, I will be glad I once did one small thing seriously. Compounding is better started early. And if you have not started yet, start with one honest game today.
