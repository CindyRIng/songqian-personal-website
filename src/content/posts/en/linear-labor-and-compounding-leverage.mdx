I was on a bus home once, stuck in traffic. I stared out of the window at a construction site and watched a worker swing a hoe: one swing, one result; another swing, another small result. It looked exhausting, and none of that effort would grow on its own.

The same day I was thinking about something else: a line of code written by an engineer might run a million times. His effort was not necessarily greater than the hoe worker's, but the output was an order of magnitude apart.

Put those two images together and the difference is not effort. It is leverage.

## Invest once, produce repeatedly

I divide work into two kinds.

One is linear labour: time traded directly for a result, which stops the moment you stop. Deliver one order, earn for one order; run one consultation, charge for one consultation. Once the hours are sold, they are gone.

The other is compounding labour: what you do now will be used again, reused, and amplified later. Writing a tool, distilling a method, building a knowledge base, maintaining a body of work that keeps accumulating — all of this belongs here.

The test is simple. In a review, ask one question: will this thing be used again later?

If yes, it is worth doing even when it pays nothing today. If no, it is pure time consumption — and getting better at it only means consuming time more skilfully.

## Three kinds of leverage

So far I can identify three.

- Intellectual leverage: proof techniques in mathematics, logical reasoning, understanding complex systems. Spending three hours on one proof looks inefficient today, but the mental framework it builds gets reused for life.
- Cognitive leverage: a knowledge base, a system of principles, journals and reviews. Every pitfall you fall into becomes a principle, and you stop paying tuition for the same class of mistake twice.
- Technical leverage: code written once runs many times; a tool built once gets used over and over. For ordinary people this is the easiest kind of leverage to acquire and the most underrated.

What unites them is: invest once, produce repeatedly. The difference is not "work harder." It is putting the effort in the right place.

## Why a job is linear

The essence of a job is wholesale time sold to an organisation: income equals hours times rate. The ceiling of that model is your time, and time has a low ceiling.

Worse, work experience is often valid only inside one organisation: the processes, the relationships, the context, the default way of doing things. Leave that environment and a lot of experience depreciates quickly.

That does not mean jobs are worthless. A job is the basis of cash flow and the fastest way to learn how an industry actually runs. But if all your energy goes there, the result is that linear labour supports you while no asset accumulates.

## What this does not mean

I am not saying everyone should quit and build a product. Most people, myself included, need a job for cash flow, for structure and for feedback from the real world. The point is not to reject linear work but to stop relying on it exclusively.

The practical version is small. If a task repeats every week, spend one hour turning it into a script, a template or a checklist. If a problem keeps coming back, write down the cause and the fix so the next person — including future you — does not have to solve it from scratch. A sales team that turns its call scripts into a tested playbook, a support team that turns recurring answers into a knowledge base: none of it is glamorous, but all of it compounds inside a linear job.

## Building a compounding system for yourself

My strategy has two layers.

First layer: support yourself. On that premise, find ways to make the paid work itself compound. Turn repeated processes into tools, turn pitfalls into checklists, turn whatever can be standardised into templates. After one year, some people only have one more year of seniority; others have a set of assets they can carry away.

Second layer: put the remaining time into non-linear growth — things that look useless now but can be reused and amplified later. Learning something closer to the fundamentals, building your own tools, publishing consistently, maintaining a knowledge base. They give you no feedback in the short term and change your options in the long term.

> In review, I ask myself one question: can what I am doing now be used later? If it can, it is worth doing even if it has no value today.

## In the end

None of this denies hard work, and it does not promise a shortcut. A compounding system sounds appealing, but its first half is usually dull: heavy input, slow feedback, no visible progress.

That dull stretch is exactly what separates people. The real value of first-principles thinking is not the posture of challenging an industry; it is the conclusion you can calculate once you return to the essence of the problem. And the calculation usually says: keep going, even when nothing echoes back.

Do you want this year to be one year, or to become the foundation of many years to come? The answer is not in a slogan. It is in where you put your time today. That is the only difference that compounds: not more hours, but hours placed where they can be used again.
