I got serious about knowledge management because my own notes let me down.

I was writing a proposal and I knew I had recorded one crucial number somewhere. I searched for half an hour: bookmarks, chat history, several documents. I finally found it in a file named "New Document (3)". That was the moment I understood: having written something down is not the same as owning it.

So I spent a while designing a system with one goal: to make sure my future self can actually take things out when needed. Here is what the system looks like today.

## Eight folders: decide where things live first

Almost all note chaos starts with "where should this go". So the first step is not writing notes, it is fixing the folders.

- Diary: the raw data layer, a record of what happens each day, the source of everything else.
- Note: the knowledge layer, structured concepts, technical points, definitions.
- Essay: the experience layer, methods, reviews, solutions to problems.
- Project: the delivery layer, documents and outputs for active and finished projects.
- Index: the retrieval layer, gathering related items scattered across folders.
- Principle: the decision layer, rules of conduct, criteria, values.
- Resource: the reference layer, third-party material, tools, external content.
- Archive: finished items that no longer need to occupy active retrieval space.

There is only one rule: the same kind of content does not live in two folders. Before creating anything, decide which layer it belongs to. It takes ten seconds, and it decides whether your knowledge base is a mess or a searchable structure three years from now.

## Naming: know what a file is without opening it

Folders solve location; naming solves recognition. All my files follow one structure:

`[prefix][YYMMDD][index] Title.md`

The prefix maps to content type: ND for a daily diary, N for a knowledge point, E for essays and methods, PJ for project documents, I for indexes, P for principles. The date is two digits each for year, month and day, and the index is the order of same-type files that day.

For example, the third essay of 13 April 2026 is called E2604133 Personal Knowledge Management Methodology Summary.

The benefits are practical: sorting by filename is sorting by time; same-day files do not collide; one glance tells you whether to open something. More importantly, backlinks get stable anchors — renaming a title does not break the links.

## Five-minute granularity: the diary is raw data

My diary is not a mood journal. It is closer to a data log.

Each event is one line: event name, place, time span, energy rating, followed by an objective description. Times are rounded to five minutes — 12:21 becomes 12:20, 12:27 becomes 12:25. Words like "around" are not allowed.

The rule sounds obsessive, but it solves a real problem: memory lies. Record within 24 hours and the details survive. Fill it in three days later and what you write is your interpretation, not the fact.

The value of the diary is not the day itself. It is the source of everything else. A review, a principle, an essay should all be traceable back to the afternoon when it first happened.

## Four kinds of knowledge atoms: C/P/M/E

When distilling knowledge, I distinguish four kinds of content and mark them with four letters.

C is concept: what it is, where its boundary lies, how it differs from similar concepts.
P is principle: what to judge by when this kind of choice appears. It comes from a concrete event but must be usable without that event.
M is method: how to do it. Steps, commands, templates, checklists — the more executable the better.
E is evidence: concrete cases, data and results that show the method or principle actually works.

Separating them produces an immediate benefit: within one topic I can see which kind is missing. Lots of concepts but no principles means I have been learning without judging. Lots of methods but no evidence means those methods may never have been verified.

> Knowledge that cannot be called into production is just a collection.

## Why plain text plus backlinks

People have asked why I do not use a prettier tool. The reason is simple: I want this to survive more than ten years.

Plain text has almost no dependencies. It will still open a decade from now, it can be read by any editor, diffed, searched, and fed directly to an AI. Screenshots and PDFs cannot do that, and they are exactly where information dies most easily.

Backlinks solve a different problem: folders are trees, knowledge is a network. A link from a diary to a principle, from a principle to a project, stops content from being chopped up by folders. From any node you can return to the original event, or move forward to where the idea was later used.

Add a layer of indexes that gather scattered items in the same domain, and retrieval no longer depends on memory.

## Maintenance: keep the system alive

A system is not finished once it is built. I have four fixed routines: daily entry, weekly consolidation, monthly archiving, quarterly review of principles.

Every day I record events and link related notes. Every week I distil reusable material from the diary into notes or essays and update the indexes. Every month I move finished projects and goals into the archive and clean out expired content. Every quarter I review the principle library, delete outdated rules, add new ones and recalibrate the criteria.

None of this is complicated, but it needs a fixed rhythm. The biggest enemy of knowledge management is not a lack of tools; it is "I will organise it when I have time." That time usually never comes.

## In the end

My understanding of knowledge management is now simple: it is not collection, it is retrieval.

A good knowledge system should actually be opened when you write a proposal, make a judgement, or review a project — and actually save you time. If it cannot do that, no matter how tidy it is, it is just a very pretty collection folder.

The system will keep evolving. Rather than chasing a perfect method, I care more about whether it is still being used.
