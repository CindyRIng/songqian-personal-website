January 11, Shijiazhuang, indoor court. I propped my phone on the fence behind the baseline and recorded a practice match.

Watching it back was unpleasant. I had always assumed my forehand was fine. On video the backswing is too big, and the contact point is often behind my body. My footwork is lazier than I thought — a lot of balls I reached for instead of moving to.

What I feel and what is true are very far apart.

## Why I started recording

This year I want to win two Tennis123 3.5 singles titles. That is a stretch; my level is still short of it.

Practising alone does not tell me what is wrong. The coach says my contact point is late and I think it is not; then I watch this match and it is late by a lot.

So I wanted something I can watch repeatedly. Video is the most direct option. No equipment to buy; a phone is enough.

## Three layers of adjustment

Watching video makes you want to fix everything at once. Change the stroke today, the tactics tomorrow, and end up with nothing changed.

I set three layers, and only touch one at a time.

Body and base state: stance height, ready position, first-step rhythm. The foundation every shot uses.

Stroke structure: contact point, follow-through, involvement of the non-dominant hand.

Pattern and tactics: ball speed, depth, net clearance, shot selection.

In that footage I assigned the problem to the "contact point" layer first, noted the rest, and left it. If you change three things, the next video cannot tell you which one did anything.

There is an order to the layers too. Body state is the most basic; if the stance is wrong, every stroke adjustment feels wrong. So I usually settle stance and first step before touching stroke structure.

## Rules for clipping

When reviewing I cut the match into clips. Three rules.

A problem has to appear at least three times to be clipped. Once could be chance. Three times is repeatable.

No clip longer than ten seconds. Nobody wants to watch a two-minute rally, including me.

One problem per session. Mixing returns, forehand and net play leaves you with no idea what to change.

Then I write an analysis: what the phenomenon is, how often it happens, in what situations. Only what I saw, not the explanation. For example: "after 1:1 in the second set, contact point three times behind the body" — not "because I was nervous".

Then choose one main cause. If there is no clear cause, write "undetermined" and change nothing this round.

Watching your own video, you instinctively make excuses: the wind, the court, the surface. I added a rule: analysis describes phenomena only. Causes are for training to test.

Clipping a match takes about forty minutes. I do it the evening after playing; watching one day later is calmer and more objective. Footage from three days later mostly never gets watched.

## Training follows the same rules

One training target per cycle, and it has to be verifiable on video.

"Forehand contact point stays in front of the body in medium-to-fast rallies" is verifiable. "Improve forehand consistency" is not — and you never know when it is done.

Success criteria have to be numbers: consecutive reps, a ratio, what counts as failure. Written before training, checked after. My most recent one: twenty fed balls, at least fourteen contacted in front of the body, two sets in a row to pass.

I also write down what is off-limits this cycle. Otherwise I wander into something else halfway through.

## Conditioning counts too

I am still doing leg rehab, every other day: single-leg stability and glute medius mostly. The coach says tennis movement comes from the legs; my old habit was standing still and reaching.

Conditioning gets logged: squats, lunges, single-leg eyes-closed balance, reps per set. The numbers are not impressive, but they show whether I cheated.

Rehab is boring. I do it at home after tennis, half-watching something, and often cut the sets short. That is the part where I fool myself easiest.

## In the match

Video review is off-court work. On court I keep one thought: how to play this point.

When I am behind, things fall apart fastest. I used to try to hit harder, and the harder I tried the more I missed. Now I stand behind the baseline for an extra two seconds, slow my breathing, then serve.

That came from the video: when I lose points in a row, the time between points before serving visibly shrinks.

## Current problems

The system reads like a complete process. Running it is a pain.

Playing alone, setting up the phone, recording, coming back and clipping takes one to two hours. Sometimes I am lazy and skip it. The sessions without video are basically wasted — afterwards all I remember is "felt okay today", with no idea what specifically did not.

Court time is another problem. Indoor courts in Shijiazhuang are hard to book; my Wednesday evening slot is booked a week ahead. When it gets taken, that week's training is gone. Last month it broke twice and the rhythm went with it.

The template has not finished a full cycle. I have only done match video, analysis, training plan. The next match's verification has not happened.

I will add it after the next match. Then I will see whether this is useful. If it is not, I will delete it — no reason to carry a pile of rules onto the court.

The video files are also a mess. A dozen matches on my phone, all named by date, no way to tell a match from a practice. One evening soon I need to rename them with opponent, score, and main problem.

I have been putting that off for two months.
