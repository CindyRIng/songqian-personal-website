People ask if writing a journal every day is tiring.

It is. But not writing is more tiring. My memory is unreliable — I often cannot reconstruct what I did a week ago, and three-day-old conversations have lost their details. At least a file lets me look back.

## I forget

This is the hardest reason of the lot. Not laziness; people just do not remember.

My memory of a week looks like this: a few very good or very bad moments, plus a fog of impressions. The ordinary days in between are blank. And the ordinary days are most of them.

Around this time last year I had the feeling of being busy all the time, and could not name a single thing I had been busy with. "I think I was busy" is a very unsteady feeling.

So the journal first solves storage. Everything else comes later.

## What I want out of the past

Remembering is not enough; I am not running an archive. I want three things.

First, not to repeat mistakes. If I fall into the same hole once, it should ideally not happen a second time.

Second, to keep the patterns that put me in a good state. The order in which I do things, which hours I am efficient in, what a good forehand feels like. Once those are lost, finding them again is expensive.

Third, to see my time honestly. I assumed I was busy. After a month of records I found I spend far more time on my phone than I thought — one week I counted a little over two hours a day, all in fragments.

None of this is news. But these were my reasons when I started, and they have not changed.

## What I actually write

Two parts.

One is a time log: what I did from when to when, where, and with whom. Very dry, like bookkeeping. Something like:

"9:00-11:30 revised the customer proposal, office, alone. 14:00-15:00 customer call, office. 20 minutes running in the evening."

The other part is what was worth mentioning that day — usually a moment with emotion in it, or a decision that mattered. That part is longer; sometimes a whole entry is just that one thing.

I keep the format minimal. Obsidian, one file per day, filename is ND plus the date, e.g. ND250529. Open it and type. I have tried templates, tags, extra fields. Each attempt lasted about two weeks before it became a chore.

## Beyond the obvious

I also keep two small things.

One is how my body feels. Which shot felt clean, which step felt right when moving. The coach's terminology rarely sticks, but "contact point in front of the body today" is something I understand two months later.

The other is conversations that moved me. Not what the other person did wrong — what I said, how my voice sounded, how they reacted. Reading it back shows when I get impatient.

Together those are less than a third of the journal, and they are what I reread most.

I also keep a separate knowledge base for methods and ideas. The journal is raw material; the knowledge base is processed. One day's entry says "customer asked about lead time three times, price did not move". A week later I write a note about sales follow-up. Without raw entries, the knowledge base runs dry.

The journal is not the destination. It is the entrance.

## What it actually does

Three things have genuinely happened.

First, things that were painful at the time turn into a few lines two months later. In early May a customer conversation went badly and I sat downstairs for a long while; the entry is half a page. Reading it a few days ago, my first reaction was "that was it?"

Second, repeated flaws become visible. I noticed that whenever I am underprepared, I talk faster, and when I talk faster I get sloppy. That pattern took three months of entries to see.

Third, I roughly know where my time goes. This is the most useful of the three.

There is a cost too. Too much detail turns into another chore; some nights at eleven, the last thing I want is to write an entry. The current rule is that three lines counts. Completeness is not the goal.

One thing I did not put in the list above: the journal is where I answer to myself. Plans written and not done are mildly embarrassing the next day. That embarrassment works better than any reminder app.

The cost is time. Ten minutes a day is sixty hours a year. Whether it is worth it — I think yes, but I cannot prove it.

## When I cannot write

The current problems are practical. Some days I am too tired and go straight to sleep, then fill in the entry the next day. Backfilled entries are visibly thinner: details are gone, and what I write is my interpretation rather than what happened.

For a stretch the journal turned into a pure log — what time I did what — with nothing to learn from it. I added one rule: every day, write at least one sentence about what went wrong. If nothing did, leave it blank. Do not manufacture.

How much journalling has actually changed me, I cannot say. Maybe it just helps me forget less. That is enough to keep going. Whether I will still keep it in five years, I do not know.
