Graduation projects are usually judged on a demo. I treated mine as an architecture exercise first, because a robot that picks up tennis balls on a court is a system problem long before it is a motor problem.

## The problem

A tennis court produces a trivial-sounding but constant chore: balls scatter after every drill, and collecting them by hand is slow and unglamorous. Commercial collectors exist, but they are expensive, heavy, and assume a flat, well-lit court. My brief was simpler and harder: design a small robot that can collect balls on a normal campus court, and design it so one student could build it over a semester.

## Five layers

The core decision was a five-layer architecture: hardware, behaviour abstraction, transport, application and view.

- **Hardware** is the chassis, two differential-drive wheels and a roller pickup mechanism, 3D-printed around off-the-shelf motors.
- **Behaviour abstraction** turns "find a ball", "approach", "pick up" into composable skills.
- **Transport** carries state and commands between the embedded controller and the on-board computer — and, during bench tests, a laptop.
- **Application** decides what to do; **view** exposes state for debugging and demos.

The point of the layers is substitutability. A behaviour should not know whether it runs against a simulated chassis or the real one, and the view should not care how the robot is built. That also produced a plan I could defend in a 3–4 month timeline: research and requirements, chassis and mechanism, control and ROS integration, vision, then integration and testing.

## What it taught me

Mechanism is a software constraint. The roller's geometry decided how fast the robot could drive, and no amount of control code fixes a bad pickup path. A layer diagram is only useful if it changes who owns a decision. And scope honesty matters: what I finished is a defensible system design with an honest prototype budget — not a polished product. The thesis is archived; the repository is not open source.
